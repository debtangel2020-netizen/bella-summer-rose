You've cast off, woven in your ends and held your finished piece up to the light. It's lovely, but maybe a few stitches look a bit wobbly, the edges curl, or a lace pattern looks scrunched up. That's where blocking comes in. It's a simple finishing step that helps your stitches relax and settle into place, and it often makes the biggest difference of all.

## What blocking does

Blocking means wetting your knitting, shaping it and letting it dry. As the fibres soak up water they loosen and even out, so tight and loose stitches start to look alike. Lace opens up, edges sit flatter and you can ease a piece to the size you want. One thing to remember: blocking can gently stretch a piece, but it can't make it smaller.

Wool and other animal fibres respond beautifully. Cotton, plant fibres and acrylic tend to soften and relax rather than reshape dramatically, which is still a nice finish.

## How to wet block, step by step

1. **Soak.** Fill a basin with lukewarm water, deep enough to cover your piece. Very hot water and lots of rubbing can felt wool, so keep it gentle. Let it soak for about 15 minutes, a little longer for chunky knits. A splash of wool wash is optional; plain water works too.
2. **Press, don't wring.** Lift the knitting out in a bundle and squeeze gently until it stops dripping. Never twist it, as that can stretch it out of shape.
3. **Towel roll.** Lay it on a clean, dry towel, roll the towel up and press on it to draw out more water. The piece should feel damp, not wet.
4. **Shape.** Lay it flat on a dry towel or a blocking mat and pat it into shape with your hands. For lace, sharp points or exact measurements, use rust-proof pins so nothing marks the fabric.
5. **Dry fully.** Leave it somewhere flat and out of the way until it's completely dry, even if that takes a day or two. Then unpin and admire.

## A few handy tips

- **Swatch first.** Some yarns, like silk, bamboo and lace-weight wools, can grow quite a bit when wet. Blocking your swatch shows you what to expect.
- **Go easy on synthetics.** Never press a hot iron on acrylic, as it can melt or go shiny. A warm soak and flat drying is the safer choice.
